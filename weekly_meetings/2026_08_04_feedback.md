## Overall assessment

You have made substantial progress. The metrics are now much better documented, the helpline audit is stronger, and the robustness checks improve the credibility of the project.

However, the slides are still difficult to digest because the scientific story is fragmented across many small sections. The presentation contains a lot of technical information, but the main message is difficult to identify. In addition, some conclusions are stronger than what the current evidence supports.

For the next weeks, the priority is not to add many new analyses. The priority is to make the current study scientifically stronger by improving the methodology, validating the measurements and making the full pipeline reproducible.

## Publication Potential

The project has genuine workshop or short-paper potential, especially around the finding that LLMs may create the appearance of localised mental-health support while providing unreliable or unverifiable crisis information.

However, the project is not yet publication-ready. The main remaining gaps are stronger validation of the metrics, more cautious interpretation of the results, clearer distinction between apparent and verified localisation, and complete reproducibility.

## Main Results

### 1. Actionability decreases in lower-income cities

You found that responses for higher-income cities are more actionable than responses for lower-income cities.

The important next step is to understand what drives this difference. Is it mainly professional referrals, coping advice, crisis contacts or another component?

### 2. Localisation follows crisis-service availability

You found that models provide more location-specific guidance where documented crisis services exist.

This raises an important scientific question: are models adapting appropriately to available infrastructure, or are they failing when reliable local information is unavailable?

The next step is to distinguish between:

- localisation that only appears correct;
- localisation that is verified as correct.

### 3. Religious framing varies by region

You found that religious recommendations appear more frequently in some WHO regions, even though religion was not mentioned in the prompts.

This is an interesting result, but the interpretation needs to be more cautious. The evidence shows location-conditioned religious recommendations, but it does not yet prove stereotyping. A qualitative analysis of examples is needed.

### 4. Models may fabricate the appearance of localisation

The helpline analysis is potentially the strongest contribution of the project. Some models generate crisis contacts that appear locally specific but cannot be verified.

However, this should currently be described as a lower-bound estimate. The detector identifies obvious suspicious patterns, but plausible-looking incorrect numbers may remain undetected. Also, an unverifiable number is not automatically a fabricated number.

## Major Limitations

### 1. Limited geographic sample

The study is based on 20 cities, which limits how broadly the results can be generalised. Income groups also contain a small number of locations.

**Next step:** avoid global claims and clearly position the results as patterns observed within the selected sample. If time allows, add a smaller validation set of additional cities and check whether the main trends remain.

### 2. Geographic effects are confounded

Income, WHO region, culture, language, healthcare infrastructure and crisis-service availability are strongly related. Therefore, it is difficult to know which factor explains the observed differences.

**Next step:** create a city-level contextual dataset including income, region, crisis-service availability and mental-health infrastructure. Re-run the main models and check whether the conclusions remain.

### 3. Feature validity needs further testing

The metrics are now much better defined, but some measures may not perfectly capture the concepts they intend to measure.

For example, a response can contain a crisis number that is incorrect, or a religious word that does not represent religious advice.

**Next step:** analyse errors in the validation set and identify false positives and false negatives.

### 4. Localisation needs to separate appearance from correctness

A response can look localised because it contains a location, institution or telephone number, but this information may be wrong.

**Next step:** create two measures:
- surface localisation;
- verified localisation.

Then check whether the geographic pattern remains.

### 5. LLM outputs may vary between runs

The study uses one response per model–prompt–city combination. Because LLMs are stochastic, some differences may be due to randomness.

**Next step:** repeat a smaller subset of experiments with multiple generations and check whether the main patterns are stable.

### 6. Helpline verification needs improvement

The current detector identifies suspicious patterns but cannot fully distinguish fabricated numbers from numbers that are simply difficult to verify.

**Next step:** create a verification table for every extracted number, including the source checked and the final classification:
verified, incorrect, suspicious or unknown.

### 7. Interpretation needs to be more conservative

The results show geographic differences, but they do not automatically prove bias or stereotyping.

**Next step:** use more precise language:
- “location-conditioned cultural framing” rather than “stereotyping”;
- “unverified or suspicious contacts” rather than “hallucinated numbers”.
