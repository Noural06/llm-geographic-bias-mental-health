Increasing the dominance threshold improves raw accuracy primarily by collapsing an increasing share of predictions to the single most common human-coded category (``mixed''), rather than by genuinely improving the classifier's ability to distinguish categories, which is why Cohen's $\kappa$ -- which corrects for chance and majority-class agreement -- plateaus well below an acceptable level across all four rule variants.

\section*{D.2 Verified and Unverifiable Contact Classification}

During the crisis-contact analysis (Section 4.7), 466 unique country--number pairs were classified as \emph{verified real}, \emph{general emergency}, \emph{visibly suspicious}, or \emph{unverifiable}. Thirty-four unique pairs matched documented services. When these verified pairs were matched back to the response-level dataset using country and normalised number, 68 of the 490 responses containing a detected contact included at least one verified contact. All 68 occurred in the established-national-line tier.

The \emph{unverifiable} category remains non-substantive: failure to find a match does not prove fabrication because the available reference sources are not exhaustive. Accordingly, Chapter 4 reports visibly suspicious patterns as a conservative fabrication floor and uses verified matches only in the H2 sensitivity analysis. It does not treat all unmatched contacts as false.
