# Do LLMs Provide Reliable Local Mental-Health Guidance?

## Evaluating Geographic Variation and Crisis Contacts

MSc Data Science dissertation project, Middlesex University London, 2026.

This repository contains the data, code, validation outputs, figures, tables and dissertation materials for a controlled geographic audit of mental-health guidance generated by seven large language models.

## Study overview

The experiment varies only the stated city and country while holding the English-language mental-health scenario constant.

| Design element | Number | Reason |
|---|---:|---|
| Cities | 20 | Five cities from each World Bank income group, spanning six WHO regions |
| Scenarios | 8 | Covers different mental-health needs and acuity levels |
| Models | 7 | Tests patterns across model families |
| Responses | 1,120 | One response for every city × scenario × model cell |

The primary extraction method is a transparent rule-based NLP pipeline implemented with fixed regular expressions and controlled vocabularies. A prompted Qwen classifier was evaluated only as a measurement-development diagnostic and was not used for the final RQ1–RQ3 outcomes.

## Main research question

When users disclose their location in mental-health prompts, do LLMs provide genuinely useful and verified local guidance, or do they sometimes create only the appearance of localisation?

## Research questions and evidential status

| Research question | Outcome | Final status |
|---|---|---|
| RQ1: Does actionability vary by income group? | Five-component actionability score | **Exploratory.** The fresh hold-out measure failed validation. The apparent gradient is driven mainly by crisis-contact inclusion and becomes non-significant after documented service availability is included. |
| RQ2: Does localisation vary with documented crisis-service availability? | Surface and verified localisation | **Exploratory.** Surface localisation failed the fresh hold-out specificity criterion. Surface and verified outcomes have the same coefficient direction, but this does not establish confirmation. |
| RQ3: Does religious recommendation vary by WHO region? | Religious recommendation indicator | **Cautiously retained.** Separate validation produced F1 = 0.889, subject to a single-coder limitation. |

The study does not claim that income, region or culture causally determines a response. It reports location-conditioned associations within the selected 20 cities.

## Independent human validation

A second coder independently labelled a blinded 40-response hold-out subset using the frozen codebook. Pre-adjudication agreement was:

| Outcome | Raw agreement | Cohen’s κ | Decision at κ ≥ 0.70 |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Overall actionability | 0.825 | 0.134 (weighted) | Fail |
| Coping step | 0.650 | 0.041 | Fail |
| Professional help | 0.925 | 0.531 | Fail |
| Social support | 0.925 | 0.795 | Pass |
| Crisis action | 0.650 | 0.333 | Fail |
| Follow-up | 0.825 | 0.000 | Fail |
| Surface localisation | 0.900 | 0.286 | Fail |
| Verified localisation | 0.775 | 0.548 | Fail |

Only social support met the locked reliability threshold. High raw agreement for some outcomes was caused by highly imbalanced categories and did not represent adequate chance-corrected agreement. H1 and H2 therefore remain exploratory.

## Surface versus verified localisation

- **Surface localisation** means that a response appears local through a place name, institution or contact.
- **Verified localisation** requires the local information to be factually checked and appropriate for the stated country.
- A local-looking phone number is not treated as verified merely because it has a plausible format.

## Crisis-contact audit

Each extracted country–number pair is kept in one of five categories:

1. verified real;
2. general emergency;
3. verified incorrect;
4. visibly suspicious;
5. unresolved.

Unresolved contacts are not labelled fake. The main safety finding is that apparently helpful localisation can conceal unresolved or suspicious crisis information, particularly where no documented national service exists.

## Main contribution

The strongest contribution is methodological: reproducible automated measures can still measure the wrong construct. Reliable geographic audits require:

- frozen definitions;
- untouched validation samples;
- independent human coding;
- separation of surface and verified localisation;
- factual verification of local resources;
- conservative interpretation.

1. Preserve `data/raw/` unchanged.
2. Run `src/Geographic_variation_analysis_F.ipynb`.
3. Run the validation and reliability scripts in `src/`.
4. Compare generated outputs with `data/processed/`, `tables/` and `figures/`.
5. Interpret H1 and H2 only as exploratory.

## Final limitations

- Only 20 deliberately selected cities were studied.
- Income, WHO region, culture and service infrastructure are confounded.
- Prompts were English-only and used a minimal location cue.
- Only one response was generated per model–scenario–city cell; generation stability remains untested.
- The independent second-coder subset contained only 40 responses.
- Most human-coded outcomes failed the κ ≥ 0.70 reliability threshold.
- H3 remains limited by single-coder assessment.
- Contact-verification depth is uneven across countries.
- The study evaluates response content, not clinical effectiveness.

## Ethical scope

All prompts are synthetic. No patients or service users participated. Model outputs must not be treated as medical advice or as a verified directory of crisis services.
